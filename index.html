    1 Traditional Methodologies

    1.1 Introduction to software development methodologies

    A software development methodology (or process model) is a structured framework that defines the tasks, milestones, deliverables, and roles needed to build and maintain high-quality software across the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) (Pressman & Maxim, 2020; Sommerville, 2016). Despite differing structures, all methodologies address four fundamental activities: specification, development, validation, and evolution (Sommerville, 2016).

    Key distinction. In plan-driven processes, all stages are planned in advance and progress is measured against that plan. In agile processes, planning is incremental and adapts readily to changing customer needs (Sommerville, 2016).

    1.2 The Waterfall model

    First described by Royce (1970) and widely adopted for large engineering projects, the Waterfall model organises the SDLC into linear, sequential phases in which each phase produces signed-off documentation before the next begins (Pressman & Maxim, 2020; Sommerville, 2016).

    • Strengths. Clear milestones, comprehensive documentation, and strong discipline for systems with stable requirements or strict safety and regulatory standards (Sommerville, 2016).
    • Limitations. Working software appears late in the project, and accommodating mid-project requirement changes requires costly rework across prior baselines (Pressman & Maxim, 2020).

    1.3 Rapid Application Development (RAD)

    Introduced by Martin (1991), Rapid Application Development (RAD) is an incremental, high-speed model that compresses delivery into short 60- to 90-day cycles by combining interactive prototyping, reusable software components, and automated Fourth-Generation/CASE tools (Pressman & Maxim, 2020).

    1. Requirements PlanningJoint JAD workshops define scope
    2. User DesignIterative screen & data prototypes
    3. Rapid ConstructionComponent assembly & testing
    4. CutoverAcceptance testing & live deployment

    When to use RAD: Ideal for modular business information systems with committed user availability. It is unsuitable when technical risks are high, performance is safety-critical, or systems cannot be modularised (Pressman & Maxim, 2020).

    2 Agile and Scrum

    2.1 Agile principles and iterative development

    To overcome the rigidity of heavyweight plan-driven models, seventeen practitioners published the Manifesto for Agile Software Development (Beck et al., 2001). Agile methodologies combine iterative (refining features through repeated cycles) and incremental (delivering working slices early) development across four core values (Beck et al., 2001; Sommerville, 2016):

    • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.
    • Working software over comprehensive documentation.
    • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation.
    • Responding to change over following a plan.

    Twelve supporting principles guide agile teams to deliver working software frequently (from a couple of weeks to a couple of months), welcome changing requirements even late in development, and maintain a sustainable pace (Beck et al., 2001).

    2.2 The Scrum framework

    Scrum is a lightweight framework for developing and sustaining complex products, founded on empirical process control—transparency, inspection, and adaptation (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). A self-managing Scrum Team comprises a Product Owner (maximises value), a Scrum Master (fosters process effectiveness), and Developers (build a usable Increment each Sprint).

    1

    Product Backlog & Sprint Planning

    The Product Backlog is an emergent, ordered list of product needs. In Sprint Planning, the team sets a Sprint Goal and selects items to form the Sprint Backlog (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).

    2

    Sprint & Daily Scrum

    A Sprint is a fixed timebox of one month or less (typically 2–4 weeks). Each day, Developers hold a 15-minute Daily Scrum to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their daily plan.

    3

    Sprint Review

    At the end of the Sprint, the Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the working Increment and adapt the Product Backlog based on market and user feedback (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).

    4

    Sprint Retrospective

    Before starting the Next Sprint, the team reflects on interactions, tools, and its Definition of Done to plan concrete quality and effectiveness improvements (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).

    3 DevOps and Methodology Comparison

    3.1 The DevOps lifecycle and CI/CD

    DevOps unites software development (Dev) and IT operations (Ops) across people, processes, and technology to deliver reliable software continuously (Microsoft, 2023; Pressman & Maxim, 2020). Its backbone is an automated CI/CD pipeline:

    Continuous Integration (CI)

    Developers merge code changes into a shared repository frequently; automated builds and test suites verify every commit to catch defects early (GitHub, n.d.; Microsoft, 2023).

    Continuous Delivery & Deployment (CD)

    Continuous Delivery automatically prepares and tests release-ready artifacts for approval; Continuous Deployment automatically pushes every passing build straight to production (Microsoft, 2023; Sommerville, 2016).

    3.3 The Fish Model (parallel verification & validation). Shaped like a fish—from requirements at the head, through analysis, design, coding, and system build along the central spine, to maintenance at the tail—the Fish Model assigns two independent teams to every phase: one conducts static verification (reviews) on the upper fin while another conducts dynamic validation (testing) on the lower fin. Though time-consuming and costly, it delivers maximum defect prevention for high-assurance systems.

    4 Conclusion and References

    No single methodology fits every software project (Pressman & Maxim, 2020; Sommerville, 2016). Traditional models such as Waterfall and the Fish Model provide predictability and rigorous documentation when requirements are stable or safety-critical, whereas RAD, Agile/Scrum, and DevOps enable rapid adaptation, stakeholder collaboration, and continuous automated delivery in dynamic environments.

    4.1 Key takeaways

    WaterfallLinear phases; best for fixed, regulated specifications.
    RADPrototypes & reusable components in 60–90 days.
    Agile & ScrumEmpirical 1–4 week Sprints and backlog adaptation.
    DevOps & CI/CDUnites Dev and Ops with automated build, test, and deploy.
    Fish ModelParallel review (verification) and test (validation) fins.
    Context rulesMatch the model to risk, team culture, and volatility.
